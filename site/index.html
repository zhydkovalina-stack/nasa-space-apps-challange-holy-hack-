The Thirsty Lakes of Konin

For 40 years a lake district in central Poland has been drying out. Now the coal mines are closing and water is coming back. Can a radar satellite help us watch the land heal?

Prototype · Wielkopolska, Poland · Landsat 1984–2025 · Sentinel-1 2024–2026 · NISAR 2026

The place

East of Poznań lies the Gniezno Lake District, a landscape of long, narrow lakes left behind by glaciers. Since the 1950s it has also been one of Poland's lignite (brown coal) regions. Open-pit mines around Konin fed the Pątnów and Konin power plants.

To dig coal from a pit, you have to pump groundwater away. This lowers the water table for kilometres around, like sucking on a straw in a juice box. And Wielkopolska is already one of the driest regions in Poland.

The JRC Global Surface Water map (1984–2021) shows exactly where the shores moved. red means it used to be water and is now dry land.

Is the mine to blame? This question is still disputed. A government expert report blamed dewatering of the Jóźwin IIB pit (2003–05) as "practically the only cause" [6]. A 2016 study found that mining alone cannot explain the drop, and pointed to drought and evaporation too [7]. Monitoring in 2024 still found the cone of depression (the dip in the water table around the mines) reaching beyond Wilczyńskie and Suszewskie [8]. The honest answer is probably both: a dry climate plus pumping.

Now: watching with NISAR

NISAR (the NASA–ISRO Synthetic Aperture Radar, launched July 2025) sends its own microwave pulses and listens for the echo. It sees through clouds, at night, every 12 days. It uses long L-band waves (24 cm) that reach through plants down to the ground and water below.

  • Black: calm water works like a mirror and bounces the pulse away.
  • White/grey: forests. Branches scatter the pulse in all directions, so both channels are bright, especially HV.
  • Magenta: fields and towns. The direct HH echo is stronger than the "depolarised" HV echo.
Why single radar images look grainy and flicker. Every radar image has speckle, a random salt-and-pepper pattern from many echoes interfering inside each pixel. On top of that, wind ripples lakes and rain wets fields from one pass to the next. Because NISAR revisits the same spot on a fixed grid, we can stack all passes and filter them together (a multi-temporal speckle filter). That keeps real changes and removes most of the noise. NISAR's 20 m pixels are the real limit of this data mode, so the sharpest close-ups are about 20 m.

NISAR measures two "flavours" of echo at once. HH sends and receives horizontal waves. HV receives the part that was twisted by branches and leaves. Drag to compare them: forests light up in HV, while lakes stay black in both.

A whole season in one picture

Instead of flipping through dates, we can paint the season into one image. Each colour channel is a different month of NISAR's HV echo: red = June green = August blue = October. Grey or white means "the same all season". Colour shows when a place changed.

Is the ground moving?

NISAR can do something no camera can: compare the exact timing of its echoes between two passes. This technique, called InSAR, reveals two things. First, how much the ground moved toward or away from the satellite, down to millimetres. Second, the coherence: how much a place stayed the same between passes.

Ground movement: a first, honest look. We chained NISAR's 12–36-day interferograms from June to October 2026 (9 pairs on track 57, 5 on track 80) to look for the ground rising as groundwater returns around the closed pits. After 4 months, the signal is not yet clear. Each pair carries 5–15 mm of atmospheric noise, fields lose coherence between passes, and the two tracks disagree at the same places. Groundwater-driven movement is slow, a few mm to cm per year. A year or more of NISAR passes and a proper time-series method (for example MintPy) should separate it from the noise. This is exactly what NISAR's regular 12-day schedule is built for.

Drought: the fields dried out

What NISAR adds

12 days
a new look at every lake and pit, through clouds, all year, including Polish winters
L-band
long waves see water under reeds, which is exactly where shrinking lakes lose their edges
200 m
soil-moisture maps on every pass. Each map square is 200 × 200 m (about 5 football pitches), detailed enough to see which fields are drying out.
mm–cm
ground movement: by comparing passes, radar can detect the land sinking or rising by just millimetres to centimetres, for example as groundwater is pumped out or comes back.

Satellites like Landsat tell us the past. NISAR is the tool for the future: an independent, free check on whether the 100+ million złoty restoration plans are working [2].

Next steps

  • Ground movement: after a full year of passes, run a proper InSAR time series (for example MintPy) to look for uplift around the closed pits as groundwater recovers. Four months were not enough (see Chapter 3).
  • Pit filling: combine both NISAR tracks with a terrain model, so that radar shadows on the steep pit walls are not counted as water.
  • Lake levels: compare NISAR water area with lake gauge readings and groundwater wells.
  • Validated data: re-run when the calibrated (VALIDATED) NISAR soil moisture is released.
Prototype caveats. NISAR products used here are provisional. Our water and soil-moisture numbers use simple thresholds and medians. They are a first exploration, not official measurements. The figures quoted from other sources are cited, and some are disputed.
