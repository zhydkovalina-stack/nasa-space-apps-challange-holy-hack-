The thirstylakes ofKonin

Coal mines drained a lake district in central Poland for 40 years. Now the mines are closing and the water is coming back. NISAR watches it every 12 days, through cloud and darkness.

Forty years of drying

To dig lignite from an open pit, you pump the groundwater away, and the water table drops for kilometres around. Wielkopolska is already Poland's driest region. Between the pumps and the dry climate, the lake chain north of the mines lost a third of its water.

Is the mine to blame? Still disputed: a government report blamed pit dewatering [1], a 2016 study pointed to drought and evaporation too [2]. Probably both.

NISAR finds the water

Calm water is a mirror for radar: the pulse bounces away and the lake goes dark. Forests scatter it back and glow. NISAR maps every lake on every pass, cloud or not, so we can count the water.

  • ForestBright: branches scatter the L-band pulse back.
  • FieldsMid: rough soil sends some energy back.
  • WaterRight side: how often each spot was water across 16 passes. Brighter = every pass.521 ha

Within one season the lakes hardly move: the decline took decades. That makes 2026 a precise baseline. With a new reading every 12 days, NISAR can show within a year or two whether restoration turns the trend around.

A coal pit becomes a lake

Mining at Jóźwin IIB stopped in 2023. Now the pumps are off and the pit is filling, on its way to an 840 ha lake. In brightness alone, water and the bare sand on the pit floor both look dark. InSAR coherence tells them apart: water changes between passes, sand stays the same.
